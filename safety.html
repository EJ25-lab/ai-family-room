🛡️ Stay safe

AI is a tool. Tools have safety rules.

We're not going to lecture you. We're going to tell you the four things that actually matter — so you can use this well without getting burned.

The principles behind the missions

We don't collect what we don't need.

Three frameworks shape how every mission is designed. They may sound formal, but they all come back to the same thing — your people's privacy stays with your people.

FERPA
Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act

Your child's education records belong to your family. We never ask you to share a school document. Every mission is designed so you can complete it without pasting a protected record into any AI.

COPPA spirit
Children's data stays with you

This room is built for adults. Anything your child does on a connected AI tool is a conversation for your home, not for us to mediate. We never collect a child's information here.

Personal details
Specifics belong with you

Our missions never need addresses, workplaces, schedules, or sensitive locations to do their job. Keep the specifics close — the missions work just as well without them.

What happens when you overshare

A few stories about information that left the family.

None of these end in disaster. They end in information leaving your family that didn't have to.

The IEP that didn't stay in the family

A parent pastes their child's full IEP into a free AI to translate the dense paragraphs. The translation comes back beautifully. Years from now, that diagnosis still lives in an account history. When their kid is older and ready to introduce themselves to a coach, a college, or a first employer — the chance to share their own story should belong to them.

✦ The mission works without the document. That's the gift.

The moving email that shared too much

A parent asks AI to "write a polite note to the landlord about our move" and adds the move-out date and the new neighborhood. The note is perfect. The dates and the new address now live somewhere they weren't meant to — a digital third place that wasn't part of the original conversation.

✦ The email lands the same way without them.

The brain dump that named the teacher

A parent organizes their thoughts for a hard meeting and includes the teacher's name and the school. The one-pager comes back exactly right. The teacher's name belonged in the meeting itself — not in a third place where someone could find it later.

✦ Save the names for the room you're walking into.

The "I'll be thorough" overshare

A parent uploads a photo of the birth certificate to prove a kid's age for a school program. Now the AI has the full legal name, the date of birth, both parents, the hospital — more information than the moment needed.

✦ The school will ask for the document the right way, on the right form.

The missions are built so it doesn't have to leave. That's the whole point of the room.
🔒 Rule 1 · The "don't share" list

Some things never go in an AI chat.

AI chats can be saved, logged, used to train future models, or breached. Treat any AI chat the way you'd treat posting on Facebook — assume someone might see it. Then live by this short list.

🔒

Social Security numbers. Bank info. Credit cards. Passwords.

Never. Not for any reason. Not even to "test" what AI would say.

🏛️

Your full home address. Kids' full names. Kids' schools.

If you need help with a school issue, say "my child's school" not the school name. Say "my neighborhood" not the street.

🎖️

Deployment dates. Specific unit names. Exact orders dates.

Use a window ("next quarter," "late summer") instead of a date. This is the OPSEC rule that protects your household and their unit.

🩺

Detailed medical records. Diagnoses tied to your name.

AI can help you understand a diagnosis or prep for an appointment — just describe symptoms or general situation, not your full medical chart.

🧑‍⚖️

Other people's private info without their consent.

Your sister's drama. Your boss's medical issue. Your kid's IEP details with their full name. Anonymize before you paste.

The one-line rule

If you wouldn't put it on a bulletin board in your hallway, church, or unit, don't put it in an AI chat.

🔎 Rule 2 · The smell test

AI can be confidently wrong.

Claude is good. It's not perfect. Read every answer with these four checks before you act on it.

1

Does it cite something specific?

If it quotes a law, a date, a court case — verify it. AI sometimes invents real-sounding sources that don't exist.

2

Does it sound too sure?

One answer with zero hedging on something complicated — medical, legal, financial — is a flag. Push back. Ask for the counter.

3

Does it match your gut?

If it contradicts what you already know to be true — trust yourself first. Ask Claude how it knows.

4

Would a human expert agree?

For medical, legal, IEP, or financial decisions — use Claude to prep for the conversation with the expert, not to replace them.

When to push back on Claude

Tell it: "Are you sure? Show me how you know that." Or: "What's the strongest argument against what you just said?" Claude will tell you when it's not sure — you just have to ask.

✋ Rule 3 · When to walk away

Some tasks are not AI tasks.

AI is great for writing, brainstorming, summarizing, drafting, translating. It is not where you go for these:

🆘

A real crisis or emergency.

If you or someone you love is in danger or thinking about self-harm — call 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or 911. Military families: the Military Crisis Line is also 988, press 1.

⚕️

Final medical decisions.

Use Claude to understand what your doctor said, prep questions, or read a lab result — but not to make the call.

⚖️

Final legal decisions.

Use Claude to understand a contract or get organized for a hearing — not to sign anything binding without a lawyer.

💰

Specific stock trades, crypto bets, or "guaranteed" anything.

AI doesn't know the future. Anyone telling you otherwise is selling you something.

🤝

Replacing the human in your corner.

Therapist. Pastor. Mentor. Best friend. AI helps. It doesn't replace the people who know you.

🎖️ Rule 4 · Military family OPSEC

For mil-spouses — the rule that protects everyone.

OPSEC isn't paranoia. It's operational security — the practice of not giving away information that could put your spouse, their unit, or your family at risk. Here's how it applies to AI.

📍

Use windows, not exact dates.

Instead of "deployment Aug 12–Nov 4," say "deployment late summer through fall." Claude can still help with logistics; the window keeps you covered.

🏛️

Use general unit names, not specific.

"Army infantry unit" is fine. "1-509 PIR Bravo Company" is not.

✈️

Don't name training exercises, downrange locations, or destinations.

"Overseas training" or "downrange" is enough for the prompt to work.

👨‍👩‍👧‍👦

Talk to your spouse before posting any AI-generated content publicly.

Especially anything that mentions deployments, training, or family adjustments. Their command may have specific rules.

The OPSEC golden rule

Would you say this to a stranger at the gym? If not, don't say it to Claude. AI is helpful — but it's still a stranger.

Now you know the rules. Go practice.
